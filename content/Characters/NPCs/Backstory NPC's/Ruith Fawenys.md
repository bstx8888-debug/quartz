## 🌿❄️ Ruith Fawenys — The Place He Chose to Remain

Ruith Fawenys was not born to Winter. He was born beneath blooming canopies and endless twilight skies, in the heart of the Spring Court, where magic moved like breath—fluid, expressive, alive. There, magic was not something to be restrained. It was something to be _felt_, to be _shared_, to be _celebrated_. And Ruith was very, very good at it.

Even as a child, his magic grew quickly—too quickly, some said. Where others coaxed vines to bloom, Ruith’s growth surged uncontrollably. Roots split stone. Branches twisted beyond intention. Once, in a moment of panic, an entire garden overtook itself in a matter of seconds—beautiful, wild, and utterly beyond his control. No one was harmed. But no one forgot.

In the Spring Court, such things were not punished. They were… redirected. Quietly. Carefully. Ruith was given new instructors. New disciplines. New expectations. “Growth must be guided,” they told him. “Expression must have purpose.” “Magic reflects the self—so the self must be shaped.” And so he learned.

He learned how to _hold back_. How to prune his instincts before they could bloom too far. How to make his magic elegant instead of overwhelming. He became, in time, exactly what the Court wanted him to be—controlled, refined, safe.

But he never forgot what it felt like—to be _too much_.

It was not his own struggle that changed him. It was a student.

Years later, Ruith had already begun to teach—young Eladrin of noble lines, each one burdened with expectation long before they understood what it meant. One of them reminded him of himself. Not in magic, exactly—but in the way it _slipped_. Spiked unpredictably. Refused to remain within the neat boundaries the Court demanded. A different kind of instability, but familiar all the same.

The instructors corrected it, of course. They always did. Tighter discipline. Stricter oversight. Less freedom. The student tried. Ruith saw that much. But something in them began to close—slowly, invisibly. Not rebellion. Not failure. Suppression. And suppression, Ruith knew, was not control. It was delay.

The breaking point came quietly. There was no great explosion. No dramatic failure. Only a moment—small, contained—where the student lost control in a way no one could ignore. No one was harmed. But this time, the Court did not redirect. They decided.

Binding was efficient. Clean. Merciful, they called it. The student would live, work, contribute. Their magic—dangerous, unpredictable, unnecessary—would be sealed away. A problem solved without bloodshed.

Ruith stood in the chamber when it was done. He said nothing. No one asked him to.

Afterward, he returned to his quarters and found he could not cast. Not because he had lost the ability—but because something in him refused. That was the first time he understood the truth. The Court did not create control. It created _fear_.

He left Spring not long after. There was no exile. No scandal. No dramatic departure. Only a quiet reassignment. The Winter Court had need of instructors. And Ruith volunteered.

Winter was… different. Where Spring allowed expression, Winter demanded precision. Where Spring nurtured growth, Winter enforced stillness. Magic here was sharpened, refined, perfected. And the students? They arrived already afraid.

“Winter is where they send the ones who must be corrected.” Ruith understood that almost immediately. These were not the worst students. They were the ones who _didn’t fit cleanly_. Too emotional. Too inconsistent. Too slow. Too fast. Too something. Too much.

So he stayed. Not because he belonged there. But because they did.

He did not fight the system directly. That would have failed. Instead, he became something within it—a quiet anomaly. An instructor whose students improved—but not in the way others expected. He did not crush instability. He _studied_ it. He did not silence mistakes. He let them happen—safely, deliberately, where they could be understood instead of feared.

He taught discipline, yes. But not as suppression. As awareness.

“Magic reflects the self,” he would say, voice calm, measured. Then, after a pause: “So perhaps we should understand the self before we try to control it.”

Some of his peers disapproved. “Fawenys doesn’t correct students,” they said. “He indulges them.” Perhaps. But his students did not break. Not the way others did.

And so Ruith Fawenys remained in Winter. Year after year. Class after class. Watching. Guiding. Intervening, when necessary—not loudly, not publicly, but always _precisely_.

He never spoke of the student who had been bound. Never spoke of the moment that changed him. But sometimes, when a lesson went too still—too perfect—he would shift it, just slightly. Introduce uncertainty. Let something _imperfect_ exist a little longer than it should.

Because he had learned, long ago, what happened when it didn’t.

Ruith did not believe he could change the Feywild. He did not believe he could dismantle the expectations of Courts or the quiet machinery of control that kept them stable. But he could do one thing, again and again, for as long as he remained.

He could stand between the system… and the students it would otherwise erase.
## ❄️🌿 **The Quiet Between Lessons**

The first time Ruith Fawenys noticed Revon Elenarion Falte, he did not look at the spell. He looked at the silence that followed it. A simple exercise—shaping frost into a suspended lattice. The other students formed clean, geometric structures, each one nearly identical. Precise. Controlled. Acceptable. Revon’s did not hold its shape. It flickered—collapsed—reformed into something almost beautiful before unraveling again into drifting motes of cold light. Not failure. Not exactly. Something else. The other instructors would have corrected the form. Reset the attempt. Noted the deviation. Ruith watched the boy instead. Watched the tension in his shoulders. The way his breath caught—not in frustration, but in anticipation. As if part of him had been waiting for it to happen. When the lattice finally dissolved, the classroom stilled. Eyes turned. Judgments formed quickly, quietly. Ruith said nothing for a long moment. Then, gently: “You’re trying to make the magic behave.” Revon stiffened, already bracing. Ruith stepped closer, not to loom—but to lower his voice. “Try listening to what it’s doing first.” That was the beginning.

Revon did not trust him. Not at first. He had already learned the rhythm of expectation: perform, correct, conceal. Every lesson was a test, every mistake a mark against him. Even kindness, when it appeared, came with conditions. Ruith offered none. He did not call attention to Revon’s inconsistencies. He did not report them. He did not fix them. Instead, he changed the shape of the lessons. Where others demanded replication, Ruith allowed variation. Where others imposed structure, he introduced questions.
   “Why do you think it shifted?”
   "What did you feel before it changed?”
   “What happens if you don’t stop it?” 
It was unsettling. And worse—it worked.

The first time Revon lost control in front of him, it was small. A pulse of color through a frost spell. A ripple that didn’t belong. It vanished quickly, but not before Revon saw it reflected in Ruith’s eyes. He froze. Waited. For correction. For consequence. For the quiet, careful dismantling of everything he was trying to hide. Ruith only studied the space where the magic had been. Then, softly: “You don’t need to hide from me.” Revon didn’t answer. But something shifted.

From that day forward, their lessons changed. Not publicly. Not enough for others to notice. But in the quiet spaces—after class, between assignments, in moments that could be dismissed as coincidence—Ruith began to guide him differently. He taught Revon how to feel the edges of his magic. Not suppress it—but recognize it before it surged. He introduced grounding techniques disguised as breathing exercises. Redirected casting framed as refinement. He never named it. Never said the word chaos. But Revon began to understand that Ruith knew. And more importantly— That he was not afraid.

“Control,” Ruith said once, as they stood alone in a frost-lined courtyard, “is not the absence of chaos.” Revon watched his own magic flicker faintly between his fingers, unstable, restless. Ruith’s voice was calm. Certain. “It is the understanding of it.” Revon swallowed. “…And if I can’t?” Ruith didn’t hesitate. “Then we learn why.”

Trust came slowly. Not in declarations—but in the absence of fear. Revon began to take risks in Ruith’s presence. Small ones at first. Letting a spell run a moment longer than he should. Allowing the strange colors to surface. Testing the boundaries of what would happen if he didn’t clamp down immediately. Ruith never punished him for it. But neither did he indulge recklessness. When Revon pushed too far, Ruith would step in—not harshly, but decisively. A hand raised. A word spoken. Magic steadied. Not erased. Steered.

The other instructors noticed… something. “Fawenys doesn’t correct students,” one remarked quietly. “He indulges them.” Perhaps. But none of their students improved the way his did. And none of them unraveled quite as quietly either. By the time of the Seasonal Change Celebration, Revon had come to rely on those quiet moments more than he realized. The lessons that weren’t written. The guidance that wasn’t spoken aloud. The understanding that he was not… broken. Just different.

Ruith almost told him, once. Almost explained everything—the risks, the consequences, the truth of what would happen if the Court ever saw him clearly. But each time, he stopped himself. Not out of fear. Out of timing. Revon was still learning to stand. Ruith would not be the one to make him run. When the blizzard came, it tore through the Spring Court without warning. A wrongness in the air. A violence that did not belong to any season. Ruith felt it immediately. Not just the storm— But the signature beneath it. Familiar. Fractured. Amplified beyond anything it should have been. His heart sank. He survived. Others did not.

And as the storm receded, leaving ruin and questions in its wake, Ruith already knew what the Court would do next. They would search for cause. They would demand answers. They would find the nearest truth they could control—And make it the truth. Revon would not survive that. Not as he was.

Ruith did not wait for certainty. Did not wait for proof. He set a message via spell. The message he sent was brief. No flourish. No explanation. Just one word:
Run.
It was the first command he had ever given him. And the last.
